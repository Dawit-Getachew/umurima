Rwanda sits in the East African highlands and shares growing conditions with Burundi, western Uganda, western Kenya and eastern DRC. Advice from these areas is often relevant, but altitude, rainfall and soil differ from place to place, so local guidance from RAB should always come first.

Most of the region has two rainy seasons, the long rains and the short rains, which is why two main cropping seasons are possible each year. Highland areas above 1800 metres are cooler and suit Irish potato, wheat, tea and climbing beans. Lower and warmer areas suit cassava, banana, rice, sorghum and drought tolerant maize.

Soil erosion is the largest long term threat on steep East African hillsides. Terraces, contour bunds, grass strips of napier or vetiver, trenches and mulch all keep soil and water on the plot. A farm that loses topsoil every rainy season will need more fertilizer each year for less yield.

Rainfall has become less predictable across the region. Practical responses are shorter duration varieties, planting only when rains are established, mulching to hold soil moisture, water harvesting into tanks or trenches, and spreading risk across more than one crop instead of relying on a single one.

Fall armyworm, cassava mosaic and brown streak, banana bacterial wilt, striga and maize lethal necrosis are regional problems that cross borders. Clean planting material, certified seed, resistant varieties and rapid reporting to extension services are the shared defences.

Post harvest loss is high across the region, often between ten and thirty percent of grain. Drying grain properly, storing it in hermetic bags, and keeping stores clean and sealed protects more food than any yield increase from the field.

Regional market prices for maize, beans and Irish potato move with the harvest calendar in neighbouring countries. Prices are usually lowest right after harvest and highest before the next one, which is why safe storage lets a farmer choose when to sell instead of selling at the worst moment.
