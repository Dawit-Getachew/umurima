Coffee, ikawa, is grown by about 400,000 smallholder families in Rwanda, mostly Arabica on small hillside plots. NAEB, the National Agricultural Export Development Board, leads the coffee sector, and most farmers sell their cherries to a coffee washing station or through a cooperative.

When coffee yields drop, the usual causes are old trees that have not been pruned or rejuvenated, tired and acidic soil, no mulch, drought stress, and pests such as antestia bugs and the coffee berry borer. Check those in that order before buying any input.

Prune coffee every year after harvest. Remove dead, diseased and crossing branches and suckers, and keep a fixed number of healthy main stems so light and air reach the branches that carry next season's cherries. Very old trees produce little; ask your cooperative or NAEB about rejuvenating them by stumping.

Mulch coffee with dry grass or crop residues, kept a hand's width away from the stem. Mulch keeps soil moist through dry spells, feeds the soil as it rots, stops erosion on slopes and reduces weeds. Add compost or well rotted manure around the drip line each season.

Coffee leaf rust shows as orange powdery spots on the underside of leaves, and badly affected trees drop their leaves and yield less the next season. Prune to let air through, keep trees well fed and mulched, and ask your cooperative or the NAEB agronomist which approved fungicide to use and when.

Antestia bugs suck the developing coffee berries. They cause the potato taste defect, a raw potato flavour that lowers the price of Rwandan coffee. Check trees often, collect and kill the bugs by hand, prune to open the canopy, and follow your cooperative on any approved spray.

The coffee berry borer is a tiny beetle that bores into the berries. Pick all ripe cherries at every round, collect berries that fall to the ground, and strip the last berries off the trees at the end of the season so the beetle has nowhere to breed.

Coffee quality is decided at picking. Pick only red ripe cherries, sort out green, dry and damaged ones, and deliver them to the washing station on the same day they are picked. Mixed or delayed cherries earn a lower price and spoil the whole lot.

NAEB announces a reference farm gate price for coffee cherries each season. Before you sell, ask your washing station or cooperative what this season's price is, so you can compare it with what a buyer offers. Selling through a cooperative also gives access to bonuses, fertilizer and training.
